Opinion

We Need More Conversations That Allow People to Disagree Well

Disagreement does not have to end a conversation. Sometimes, the ability to listen, question, and respond respectfully is what makes a conversation worthwhile.

Disagreement Is Part of Conversation

People do not see everything in the same way. We come from different experiences, families, communities, professions, and circumstances. It is natural that those differences produce different ideas and opinions.

Yet disagreement is often treated as if it were a problem that must immediately be eliminated. When two people reach different conclusions, the conversation can quickly become less about understanding the issue and more about proving who is right.

There is another possibility: people can disagree and still remain willing to listen to each other.

A productive conversation does not require two people to reach the same conclusion. It requires them to be willing to understand how the other person reached theirs.

Disagreement Does Not Have to Become Disrespect

There is an important difference between challenging an idea and attacking the person expressing it.

An argument about an idea can involve questions, evidence, examples, and alternative explanations. Personal attacks change the subject. Instead of examining the idea, they focus attention on the person.

Once a conversation becomes personal, people often stop listening. They begin preparing their next response instead of trying to understand what is being said.

Listening Is More Than Waiting to Speak

Good conversation requires more than taking turns talking. Listening means making an effort to understand what another person actually means before responding.

That can be difficult when we strongly disagree. We may hear a statement and immediately begin constructing a counterargument. But sometimes the first response should be a question.

  • What makes you see it that way?
  • What experience led you to that conclusion?
  • What part of the issue matters most to you?
  • Is there something I may be misunderstanding?

These questions do not mean that we have accepted another person's position. They simply create space to understand it.

We Can Change Our Minds

There is also value in remembering that changing one's mind is not necessarily a failure. Sometimes new information exposes a weakness in an earlier position. Sometimes another person's experience reveals something we had not considered.

If every conversation is treated as a competition, changing our minds can feel like losing. If conversation is treated as a way of learning, changing our minds can become part of the process.

A person can be confident in what they believe while remaining open to better information.

Not Every Disagreement Needs a Winner

Some conversations are approached as though someone must win and someone must lose. But many disagreements are more complicated than that.

Two people can recognize different parts of the same problem. One person may notice a risk that another person overlooked. Another may have an experience that changes how the issue appears.

The result does not always have to be a winner. Sometimes the useful result is a clearer understanding of the issue.

Respect Makes Difficult Conversations Possible

Respect does not mean avoiding difficult subjects. In fact, respect can make difficult subjects easier to discuss because people know they can express a view without being immediately dismissed.

This does not mean every statement should go unchallenged. Questions and criticism are part of healthy discussion. The difference is in how they are delivered.

We do not have to lower the standard of our arguments in order to raise the standard of our conversations.

There Is Something to Learn From Disagreement

Even when a disagreement does not change our position, it can reveal how other people understand the world. That information can be valuable.

Disagreement can expose assumptions we did not realize we were making. It can reveal missing information. It can force us to explain why we believe something rather than simply repeating that we believe it.

In that sense, disagreement can strengthen an idea—or show us where the idea needs more thought.

Conversation Should Leave Room for People

The goal of a meaningful conversation should not always be agreement. Sometimes it is understanding. Sometimes it is learning. Sometimes it is simply giving two people the opportunity to express their reasoning clearly.

We can disagree about ideas while continuing to treat one another as people worthy of being heard.

That kind of conversation requires patience. It requires curiosity. It requires the ability to say, “I see it differently,” without turning those words into, “You are the problem.”

Make Room for Better Conversations

A world without disagreement is unrealistic. A world in which disagreement is handled with greater patience and respect is something people can work toward.

We do not need to agree with everyone. We do not need to abandon our convictions. But we can become better at asking questions, listening carefully, separating people from ideas, and allowing room for another perspective.

Perhaps the measure of a good conversation is not whether everyone leaves thinking the same thing. Perhaps it is whether everyone leaves having understood a little more than they did before.
